01Summary

  • Recording is the hero. Teachers see it as self-improvement and want to replay their own lessons, not only read feedback.
  • Monitoring is the deal-breaker. If directors or ECDOs can listen, teachers say they will "watch their words" and lose class control.
  • Context sets input mode. Typing in class so learners don't hear; voice when preparing alone.
  • Kenyan, curriculum-grounded answers earn trust, in English and Kiswahili.
  • Familiarity with the lesson plan decides ease. Public teachers found the AI layer straight away; private teachers new to the lesson plan were overwhelmed.

04Day 1: First impressions

EIDU_School_AI_v1.htmlWed 18 NovKasarani & Mwiki7 private teachersFocus group + 1:1
  • Recording was well received as a way to improve their teaching and get actionable tips.
  • Voice notes over typing, matching WhatsApp habits and avoiding spelling friction.
  • The screen felt overwhelming. It was their first time seeing the digitised lesson plan, so its elements and the AI layer competed for attention.
  • Privacy concern: if directors listened to recordings, teachers would perform for the recording instead of teaching naturally.
  • Control matters: they liked that recording is self-initiated and they choose which lessons to record.

05Day 2: Exploring the assistant

EIDU_School_AI_v2.htmlThu 19 NovKasarani & MwikiSame 7 private teachersLive AI responses
  • Recording stayed the favourite. Teachers want to replay their own recording to reflect on tone of voice.
  • Typing in class, voice when preparing alone. Speaking to the device in class could make learners doubt the teacher's expertise. Teachers sitting close together also avoided voice during the session.
  • Voice accuracy worry, from YouTube and Google voice search mishearing them.
  • "If I speak, it should speak back." Voice input set an expectation of spoken replies. They prefer WhatsApp video calls to voice notes because tone without a face can be misread.
  • Kiswahili worked. Kiswahili input returned Kiswahili or English depending on the question.
  • Relatable answers with Kenyan examples.
  • High expectations: generate and print images as lesson materials; answers should be visual, not text-heavy.
  • Teaching context: when stuck, teachers turn to the lesson plan, mentors, peers, other directors and some to ChatGPT. Lessons go wrong mainly from poor preparation, rushing without oral checks, and chaotic classes.

06Day 3: Public school teachers

EIDU_School_AI_v3.htmlMon 23 NovMachakos County10 public teachersGroup + hands-on
  • Easy to pick up. Little help needed; teachers named Get lesson feedback and Ask the assistant as the difference from their app.
  • Stop/Submit confusion. Teachers tapped Pause expecting it to stop. The blue submit arrow made sense only once they linked it to WhatsApp's send.
  • Voice input won them over once tried: it handled English, Kiswahili and their accents.
  • Trusted answers, grounded in the curriculum and Kenyan context, including videos.
  • What they asked: preparing lesson materials, how to make them, songs to engage learners.
  • Loved learner insights: stats on learner abilities and progress, linked to remedial lesson plans.
  • Recording: love it, fear monitoring. If ECDOs hear recordings without context, being firm with learners can sound harsh. Some saw upsides (accountability, proof of teaching, coaching) as long as it is not used to monitor them.
  • Already searching in class: some Google during lessons to answer learner questions.

09Recommended changes before build

  1. Private by default. Recordings and feedback visible only to the teacher; any sharing is opt-in and labelled as coaching. Say this in the product.
  2. Clear recording controls. One obvious Stop / Finish action, so Pause isn't mistaken for Stop.
  3. Replay. Let teachers listen back to their own recording next to the feedback.
  4. Input by context. Typing prominent for in-class use; voice for prep with a transcription preview. Explore spoken replies.
  5. Visual answers. Shorter text, images, videos and printable materials.
  6. Light onboarding. First-use guidance, especially for teachers new to the lesson plan; reduce competing on-screen elements.
  7. Learner insights. Pursue ability and progress stats linked to remedial lessons.
